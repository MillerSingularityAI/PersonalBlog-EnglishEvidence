This evidence corresponds to the chronicle writing activity from the AA1 guide (GA2-240202501-AA1-EV03). The exercise focuses on narrating past events using appropriate tense structures, cohesive devices, and academic vocabulary in English.

// Context and Objectives

The main objective of this activity was to produce a written chronicle — a genre that combines narrative and descriptive techniques to recount real events in a coherent, time-ordered sequence. This required careful use of past simple, past continuous, and past perfect tenses, as well as connectors of time and sequence such as first, then, meanwhile, subsequently, and finally.

// Process and Reflections

During the writing process, I focused on maintaining a formal yet accessible register appropriate for academic purposes. I paid particular attention to paragraph unity — ensuring each paragraph developed a single main idea — and to the use of transitional expressions that guide the reader through the timeline of events.

One of the key challenges was balancing descriptive detail with conciseness. Academic chronicles must be informative without becoming repetitive, which required multiple revision cycles to select only the most relevant details.

// Learning Outcomes

Completing this activity strengthened my ability to: (1) organize information chronologically in written English; (2) apply appropriate narrative tenses; (3) use cohesive devices effectively; and (4) revise drafts critically for clarity and coherence. This evidence reflects my progress toward B1-level written production as defined by the CEFR.

// Critical Thinking & Media Literacy

📄 EMBEDDED ACADEMIC ARTICLE

Media Literacy and Critical Thinking in the AI Era — full text available in the post below.

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly generative AI systems, has fundamentally transformed how information is produced, distributed, and consumed. In this context, media literacy and critical thinking have become essential competencies for navigating a complex and often misleading digital environment. Media literacy is no longer limited to the ability to read and interpret content; it now involves the capacity to critically evaluate AI-generated information, understand algorithmic processes, and recognize biases embedded in digital systems.

At the same time, critical thinking — defined as the ability to analyze, evaluate evidence, and make reasoned judgments — must evolve to address the unique challenges posed by AI technologies. Traditional skills such as identifying credible sources or detecting bias are no longer sufficient when dealing with synthetic media, deepfakes, and AI-generated text that can convincingly mimic human communication.

// The Transformation of Media Literacy in the AI Era

Artificial intelligence has significantly altered the media ecosystem by enabling the large-scale generation of content. This includes news articles, social media posts, images, and videos, often indistinguishable from human-created material. As a result, scholars argue for the development of Critical AI Literacy (CAIL), which integrates media literacy with an understanding of how AI systems function and how they may fail.

This shift reflects a broader transformation: individuals must now critically evaluate not only the content itself but also the processes and systems that generate it. AI tools can amplify misinformation, automate propaganda, and create echo chambers, making it more difficult for users to distinguish truth from falsehood.

// Case Study 1: AI-Enhanced Media Literacy in Education

A 2025 mixed-methods study investigated the impact of an AI-supported media literacy program implemented with primary school students. The intervention integrated generative AI tools into classroom activities and followed a structured instructional model. Results showed significant improvements in students' media literacy skills, including their ability to verify sources, understand online ethics, and critically evaluate digital content.

Qualitative findings further revealed that students developed stronger awareness of digital safety and ethical behavior, demonstrating that AI — when used appropriately — can enhance rather than hinder critical thinking. This case highlights the potential of AI as an educational tool that supports active, reflective learning.

// Case Study 2: Interactive Simulations and AI Literacy

Another study involving over 600 participants explored whether interactive simulations could improve AI literacy and critical thinking. Participants engaged in inquiry-based learning, testing hypotheses and observing AI behavior in controlled environments. The results indicated that interactive approaches significantly enhanced understanding of key concepts such as bias, fairness, and data representation, as well as improved confidence in evaluating AI systems.

This research demonstrates that experiential learning methods are more effective than passive consumption of information in developing critical thinking skills in the AI context.

// Case Study 3: AI Tools and the Risk of Cognitive Decline

While AI can support learning, several studies raise concerns about its potential negative impact on critical thinking. Research involving students and AI-assisted writing tasks found that users relying heavily on AI tools exhibited lower cognitive engagement, reduced memory retention, and diminished originality compared to those who worked independently.

Similarly, large-scale research has shown that frequent use of AI tools may lead to cognitive offloading — where individuals rely on technology instead of engaging in analytical thinking. This can result in a measurable decline in critical thinking abilities over time.

These findings suggest that uncritical reliance on AI may create an illusion of competence — often referred to as "false mastery" — without genuine understanding or skill development.

// The Dual Role of AI: Tool and Threat

The relationship between AI and critical thinking is inherently paradoxical. On one hand, AI can enhance learning, provide personalized feedback, and support complex problem-solving. On the other hand, it can reduce cognitive effort, encourage passive consumption, and propagate misinformation.

Recent research conceptualizes critical thinking in AI use as involving three key components: verification, reflection, and motivation. Individuals who actively verify AI outputs, reflect on their implications, and remain motivated to question results are more likely to use AI responsibly and effectively. This suggests that the key challenge is not the presence of AI itself, but how individuals interact with it.

// Implications for Education and Society

The rise of AI demands a redefinition of educational frameworks. Traditional literacy models must evolve to incorporate AI literacy, emphasizing not only content analysis but also system-level understanding. Educational programs should prioritize:

  • Source verification and fact-checking in AI-generated content
  • Understanding algorithmic bias and limitations
  • Ethical use of AI technologies
  • Active, inquiry-based learning approaches

Research indicates that media literacy education significantly contributes to improved critical thinking and ethical decision-making in digital environments. Furthermore, fostering these competencies is essential for democratic participation, as misinformation and algorithmic manipulation can influence public opinion and societal outcomes.

// Conclusion

Media literacy and critical thinking are no longer optional skills in the AI era; they are fundamental requirements for navigating modern digital life. The evidence shows that AI can both enhance and undermine these skills, depending on how it is used. Educational interventions, particularly those that integrate AI responsibly and emphasize active learning, can significantly improve individuals' ability to critically engage with information.

However, without proper guidance and critical awareness, AI risks creating passive users who rely on automated outputs rather than engaging in independent reasoning. The future of media literacy lies in developing a hybrid competence — one that combines traditional critical thinking with a deep understanding of AI systems. Only through this integrated approach can individuals effectively navigate the complexities of the AI-driven information landscape.

// Crime and Punishment

Video Writing Discussion

Video-based activity exploring the topic of crime and punishment in English-speaking contexts. Develops vocabulary, comprehension, and discussion skills around complex social issues.

This activity used video resources to explore the theme of crime and punishment from sociological and linguistic perspectives. Watching authentic English-language content reinforced listening comprehension and exposed me to specialised vocabulary related to law, justice, and ethics.

// Topic Overview

Crime and punishment are central to any society's legal and moral framework. In English-speaking academic contexts, discussing this topic requires familiarity with specific terminology, including: deterrence, rehabilitation, incarceration, recidivism, capital punishment, restorative justice, and penal reform.

// Vocabulary and Language Development

The video provided rich exposure to formal and semi-formal English as used in documentary and journalistic genres. I noted the use of passive constructions (criminals are sentenced, prisoners are held) and reporting verbs (researchers argue, studies suggest, critics claim), both characteristic of academic discourse on social topics.

// Discussion Task Reflection

Post-viewing tasks required constructing arguments and expressing opinions about justice systems. I practised argumentative discourse markers such as on the one hand / on the other hand, it is widely accepted that, and one could argue that.

// Critical Response

Engaging with this topic in English challenged me to think about cultural differences in approaches to justice. Different countries have fundamentally different philosophies about the purpose of punishment — whether it should prioritise deterrence, retribution, rehabilitation, or social protection. Discussing these differences in a second language required precision, nuance, and sensitivity in word choice.

This activity ultimately showed me that advanced language learning is inseparable from engaging with complex ideas. The richer and more challenging the topic, the more linguistic growth is possible.

// Final Reflection

This story reveals that in a world increasingly controlled by artificial intelligence, the line between crime and responsibility becomes blurred. When decisions are delegated to machines, human accountability does not disappear — it only becomes more difficult to identify. The tragedy is not only the loss of an innocent life, but also the absence of clear consequences for those involved. In my view, true justice cannot exist without responsibility, and technology should never replace human judgment in critical decisions. Otherwise, we risk creating a system where no one is truly guilty, and therefore, no one is punished.
